Preventing or Delaying Type 2 Diabetes

Research on structured lifestyle-change programs has repeatedly found that
modest, sustained changes can meaningfully lower the chance that someone
with prediabetes goes on to develop type 2 diabetes. Commonly cited targets
from public-health prevention programs include:

- Losing roughly 5 to 7 percent of body weight if starting above a healthy
  weight range (for example, about 10 to 14 pounds for someone who weighs
  200 pounds), achieved gradually rather than through crash dieting.
- Getting at least 150 minutes per week of moderate-intensity physical
  activity, such as brisk walking, spread across most days of the week.
- Choosing more whole grains, vegetables, fruits, and lean proteins, while
  reducing intake of sugary drinks and highly processed foods.
- Reducing prolonged sitting time and building movement into the day where
  possible.

Structured group-based lifestyle change programs, often modeled on the
Diabetes Prevention Program (DPP) research trial, have shown that these
kinds of changes can reduce the relative risk of progressing from
prediabetes to type 2 diabetes substantially over several years, in some
studies by around half, particularly for participants who reach their
weight and activity goals.

Screening Recommendations (General Public-Health Guidance)

National guidance generally recommends that adults begin routine diabetes
screening around age 35, or earlier if they have risk factors such as
overweight/obesity, a family history of diabetes, high blood pressure,
a history of gestational diabetes, or belonging to a higher-risk racial or
ethnic group. Screening is usually done with a blood test, such as a fasting
plasma glucose test, an oral glucose tolerance test, or an A1C test, which
reflects average blood sugar over roughly the past three months.

Commonly cited general reference ranges for A1C are:
- Below 5.7 percent: normal range
- 5.7 percent to 6.4 percent: consistent with prediabetes
- 6.5 percent or higher on two separate tests: consistent with diabetes

These are general population reference ranges from public-health sources,
not a diagnosis. Only a healthcare provider, using an individual's full
test results and history, can diagnose prediabetes or diabetes.

This is general public-health information only and does not replace advice
from a qualified healthcare provider.
