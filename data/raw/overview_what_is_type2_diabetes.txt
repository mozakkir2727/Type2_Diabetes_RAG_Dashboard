What Type 2 Diabetes Is

Type 2 diabetes is a chronic condition that affects how the body turns food
into energy. Normally, the pancreas releases insulin, a hormone that helps
glucose (sugar) from food move out of the bloodstream and into cells, where
it is used for energy. In type 2 diabetes, the body either does not produce
enough insulin or the cells stop responding to insulin normally, a state
often called insulin resistance. Over time this causes glucose to build up
in the blood instead of being absorbed by cells, which can lead to serious
health problems if left unmanaged.

Type 2 diabetes is the most common form of diabetes, accounting for the
large majority of diagnosed cases worldwide. It typically develops gradually
over years, and many people have it for a long time before symptoms appear
or before they are diagnosed.

Common Risk Factors

Risk factors generally include being overweight or having obesity, being
age 45 or older, having a parent or sibling with type 2 diabetes, being
physically inactive, having had gestational diabetes during pregnancy, and
belonging to certain ethnic and racial groups that carry higher population
level risk, including Black, Hispanic/Latino, American Indian, and some
Asian American and Pacific Islander populations. Having prediabetes, a
condition where blood sugar is elevated but not yet high enough for a
diabetes diagnosis, is also a major risk factor for eventually developing
type 2 diabetes.

Common Symptoms

Symptoms can include increased thirst, frequent urination, unusual fatigue,
blurred vision, slow-healing sores, and unexplained weight loss. Because
symptoms can be mild or develop slowly, some people do not notice them for
years.

General Management Approaches

Management commonly combines lifestyle changes (nutrition, physical
activity, weight management), blood sugar monitoring, and, when needed,
medications prescribed by a healthcare provider, which may include oral
medications or insulin. Treatment plans are individualized by a person's
own healthcare team based on their health history, lab results, and other
personal factors. This document intentionally does not provide dosing or
medication-adjustment guidance, since that requires a licensed clinician
who knows the individual patient.

This is general public-health information only and does not replace advice
from a qualified healthcare provider.
