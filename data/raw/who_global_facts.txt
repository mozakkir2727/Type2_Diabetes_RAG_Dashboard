Global Scale of Diabetes

Diabetes has become one of the most common noncommunicable diseases
worldwide, with the number of adults living with diabetes having risen
substantially over recent decades, driven largely by increases in type 2
diabetes. Low- and middle-income countries have seen the sharpest relative
increases in recent years, partly linked to rapid changes in diet, activity
levels, and urbanization.

Diabetes is associated with a significantly increased risk of complications
when blood sugar is not well managed over time, including cardiovascular
disease, kidney damage, nerve damage, and eye damage that can affect vision.
Because of this, early detection through screening and consistent
management are emphasized as major global public-health priorities.

Diabetes-Related Complications (General Information)

- Cardiovascular: adults with diabetes generally have a higher risk of heart
  disease and stroke compared with adults without diabetes.
- Kidneys: diabetes is a leading contributor to chronic kidney disease and
  kidney failure globally.
- Eyes: prolonged high blood sugar can damage the small blood vessels in the
  retina, a condition called diabetic retinopathy, which is a leading cause
  of vision loss in working-age adults in many countries.
- Nerves and circulation: nerve damage (neuropathy) and reduced blood flow,
  especially in the feet, can increase the risk of injuries and infections
  that are slow to heal.

Public-Health Response

International guidance generally emphasizes a combination of population-
level prevention (encouraging healthy diets and physical activity, reducing
tobacco and harmful alcohol use), improving access to affordable screening
and treatment, and training healthcare systems to manage diabetes and its
complications consistently.

This is general public-health information only and does not replace advice
from a qualified healthcare provider.
